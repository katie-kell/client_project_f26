Starting Your Experience

Start on the Same Page

You have done the prep, asked some questions, and learned more about the project. Now it is time to actually get started. Before jumping into the work, make sure your team and partner are on the same page about what you are doing and why.

A little structure at the beginning can save you a lot of confusion later. Talk about expectations, responsibilities, communication, and what success should look like before your team gets too far into the project.

Get to Know Your Partner

Your partner knows their organization better than you do. Take time at the beginning of the project to understand what they actually need and what they hope to get out of working with your team.

Do not be afraid to ask questions. The goal is not to walk into the first meeting with all the answers. It is to leave with a better understanding of the people, organization, and problem you are working with.

  • Learn about the organization and what they do.
  • Ask why this project is important to them.
  • Figure out who your main point of contact will be.
  • Ask how involved they want to be throughout the project.
  • Learn who else might be affected by your work.

Figure Out Who's Doing What

Team projects get confusing fast when no one knows who is responsible for what. Talk about roles early so everyone knows what they are responsible for and where they can go for help.

  • Decide who will be responsible for different parts of the project.
  • Choose who will be the main contact with your partner.
  • Talk about everyone's strengths and what they want to work on.
  • Decide how your team will make decisions.
  • Make sure the workload feels realistic for everyone.

Set Expectations Early

You and your partner might have completely different ideas about what the final project should look like. That is why it is important to talk about expectations before getting too deep into the work.

Be clear about what your team can realistically accomplish. A smaller project that actually solves the right problem is better than promising something your team cannot finish.

  • What is the main goal of the project?
  • What does your partner expect your team to deliver?
  • What does success look like to them?
  • Are there important deadlines or constraints?
  • What is not included in the scope of the project?

Make a Plan

Once everyone understands the goal, turn it into an actual plan. You do not need to predict every part of the project, but your team should have an idea of where you are going and what needs to happen next.

  1. Define the goal: Write down what your team is actually trying to accomplish.
  2. Break it down: Turn the larger project into smaller tasks and deliverables.
  3. Assign responsibilities: Decide who is responsible for each part of the work.
  4. Set milestones: Create smaller deadlines so everything is not happening at the end.
  5. Check in: Make time to look at your progress and adjust the plan when things change.

Decide How You'll Communicate

Good communication does not mean sending your partner a message every day. It means everyone knows when, where, and how important updates will happen.

  • Choose how your team will communicate with each other.
  • Ask your partner how they prefer to be contacted.
  • Decide how often you will meet or send updates.
  • Keep track of important decisions and feedback.
  • Speak up early when something changes or goes wrong.

Questions to Ask at the Start

You probably will not know everything after your first meeting. These questions can help your team figure out what still needs to be discussed.

  • What does success look like for this project?
  • Who are we designing or working for?
  • What is most important to our partner?
  • What are the biggest constraints we should know about?
  • What decisions can our team make on our own?
  • What decisions should involve our partner?
  • How often should we check in?
  • How should we handle changes to the project?

You're Ready to Get to Work

Your plan does not need to be perfect. Projects change as you learn more, get feedback, and run into new challenges. What matters is that your team has a clear starting point and knows how to communicate when something needs to change.
